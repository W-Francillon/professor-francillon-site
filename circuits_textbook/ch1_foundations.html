Chapter 1: Foundations of DC Circuit Analysis

ELT 112 / ENS 233 — Department of Engineering Science

Author

Wesley Francillon, PhD

0.1 🎯 Chapter Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  1. Define and quantify electric charge (\(q\)), current (\(i\)), voltage (\(v\)), resistance (\(R\)), and power (\(P\)) in DC networks using proper SI units and engineering prefixes.
  2. Apply the Passive Sign Convention (PSC) to determine whether a circuit element is absorbing or supplying electrical power.
  3. Distinguish between ideal and non-ideal (practical) sources and model the impact of internal source resistance on terminal voltage under load.
  4. Formulate Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL) and Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL) equations for single-loop and single-node-pair circuits.
  5. Analyze series resistive circuits and apply the Voltage Divider Rule (VDR).

1 What is an Electrical Circuit?

An electrical circuit is a mathematical and physical interconnection of electrical elements arranged in one or more closed paths to perform a desired function—whether that function is delivering bulk power to an industrial motor, conditioning a microvolt sensor signal, or illuminating navigation lights on a vessel.

For steady Direct Current (DC) to flow, two fundamental physical conditions must be met: 1. A Driving Potential (Source): An electromotive force (EMF) or voltage source must establish a potential energy difference across the system. 2. A Complete Closed Path (Loop): Charge carriers must have an uninterrupted conductive path leaving one terminal of the source, passing through the load, and returning to the opposite terminal of the source.

  • Closed Circuit: An uninterrupted conductive loop allowing current \(i > 0\) to flow.
  • Open Circuit: A break in the conductive path (\(R \to \infty\)), resulting in zero current flow (\(i = 0\text{ A}\)), though a nonzero potential difference (\(v \neq 0\text{ V}\)) can exist across the open terminals.
  • Short Circuit: A direct, near-zero resistance connection (\(R \approx 0\ \Omega\)) between two points, resulting in zero voltage drop (\(v = 0\text{ V}\)) across the short and potentially hazardous overcurrent if unmitigated by a fuse or circuit breaker.

2 Fundamental Electrical Quantities

2.1 Electric Charge (\(q\)) and Current (\(i\))

At the atomic level, all electrical phenomena arise from electric charge (\(q\)), measured in Coulombs (\(\text{C}\)). A single electron carries a negative charge of \(-e = -1.602 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}\), meaning it takes approximately \(6.24 \times 10^{18}\) electrons to accumulate \(1\text{ C}\) of charge.

When charge carriers are forced to move through a conductor cross-section, their time rate of flow defines electric current (\(i\)):

\[i(t) = \frac{dq(t)}{dt} \quad \left[\text{Amperes, A} = \frac{\text{Coulombs}}{\text{second}}\right]\]

For steady Direct Current (DC), where the rate of charge flow is constant over time (\(I\)), this simplifies to:

\[I = \frac{Q}{t} \iff Q = I \cdot t\]

ImportantConventional Current vs. Electron Flow

In engineering circuit analysis, we universally use Conventional Current Flow, defined as the net flow of positive charge. In metallic conductors (like copper wire), negative electrons physically drift from the negative terminal toward the positive terminal, meaning conventional current flows from positive (\(+\)) to negative (\(-\)) outside the source—opposite to physical electron drift. Always draw and solve your schematics using conventional current!

2.2 Voltage (\(v\)) — Potential Difference

Charge will not move systematically without an external force doing work on it. Voltage (\(v_{ab}\)), also called electromotive force or potential difference, is the energy (work \(w\), in Joules) required to move a unit positive charge (\(1\text{ C}\)) between two points \(a\) and \(b\):

\[v_{ab} = \frac{dw}{dq} \quad \left[\text{Volts, V} = \frac{\text{Joules}}{\text{Coulomb}}\right]\]

Mathematically, \(v_{ab} = v_a - v_b\) represents the potential of terminal \(a\) with respect to terminal \(b\). If \(v_{ab} = +12\text{ V}\), terminal \(a\) is \(12\text{ V}\) higher in potential than terminal \(b\), and therefore \(v_{ba} = -v_{ab} = -12\text{ V}\).

2.3 Resistance (\(R\)) and Ohm’s Law

As charge carriers move through a material, they collide with the atomic lattice, impeding their flow and converting electrical energy into thermal energy (heat). This physical opposition to current is Resistance (\(R\)), measured in Ohms (\(\Omega\)).

For a uniform cylindrical or rectangular conductor of length \(\ell\) and cross-sectional area \(A\), physical resistance is governed by the material’s resistivity (\(\rho\), in \(\Omega\cdot\text{m}\)):

\[R = \rho \frac{\ell}{A}\]

In 1827, Georg Simon Ohm established that for linear conductive materials at constant temperature, the voltage across a resistor is directly proportional to the current flowing through it:

\[v = i \cdot R \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad i = \frac{v}{R} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad R = \frac{v}{i}\]

The reciprocal of resistance is Conductance (\(G\)), which measures how easily a material conducts current, expressed in Siemens (\(\text{S}\)):

\[G = \frac{1}{R} = \frac{i}{v} \quad [\text{S} \text{ or } \Omega^{-1}]\]

2.4 Electrical Power (\(P\)) and the Passive Sign Convention

Power (\(p\)) is the time rate of expending or absorbing energy, measured in Watts (\(\text{W}\)). By applying the chain rule to our definitions of voltage and current:

\[p = \frac{dw}{dt} = \left(\frac{dw}{dq}\right) \cdot \left(\frac{dq}{dt}\right) = v \cdot i \quad \left[\text{Watts, W} = \frac{\text{Joules}}{\text{second}}\right]\]

For a purely resistive element (\(v = iR\)), we can substitute Ohm’s Law into the power equation to yield the three forms of Joule’s Law (always positive for a positive resistor \(R\)):

\[P = V \cdot I = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R}\]

2.4.1 The Passive Sign Convention (PSC)

How do we know whether an element (like a battery or voltage source) is absorbing power (charging/dissipating) or supplying power (discharging) to the rest of the circuit? We use the Passive Sign Convention:

  • Case 1 (Passive Alignment): If the reference current arrow \(i\) enters the positive (\(+\)) terminal of an element, calculate \(P = +v \cdot i\).
  • Case 2 (Active Alignment): If the reference current arrow \(i\) enters the negative (\(-\)) terminal (exits the \(+\) terminal), calculate \(P = -v \cdot i\).

Once computed with the proper sign: * If \(P > 0\): The element is absorbing (dissipating) power. * If \(P < 0\): The element is supplying (delivering) power to the circuit.

In any closed electrical circuit, Conservation of Energy dictates that the algebraic sum of power across all elements is strictly zero:

\[\sum P_{\text{absorbed}} + \sum P_{\text{supplied}} = 0\]

2.5 🛑 Concept Check 1.1: Physical Sizing, Power & Marine DC Wiring

Note🚤 Real-World Engineering Application: Marine Auxiliary Bus Wiring

Suppose you are wiring a pair of LED spreader lights mounted on the T-top of a 22-foot center-console boat. The spreader lights draw a steady total current of \(I = 4.0\text{ A}\) from a \(12.8\text{ V}\) \(\text{LiFePO}_4\) auxiliary battery located in the center console.

To reach the T-top and return to the negative ground bus, the total round-trip length of 14 AWG marine tinned copper wire is \(\ell = 6.0\text{ m}\) (\(3.0\text{ m}\) positive run + \(3.0\text{ m}\) negative return). The cross-sectional area of 14 AWG wire is \(A = 2.08 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}^2\), and the resistivity of copper is \(\rho = 1.72 \times 10^{-8}\ \Omega\cdot\text{m}\).

Interactive Self-Check 1.1: Wire Voltage Drop & Lamp Power Foundational

  1. Calculate the total round-trip resistance \(R_{\text{wire}}\) of the 14 AWG copper cable in milliohms (\(\text{m}\Omega\)).
  2. Calculate the voltage drop \(V_{\text{drop}}\) across the wiring harness in Volts (\(\text{V}\)).
  3. What is the actual terminal voltage \(V_{\text{load}}\) delivered across the LED spreader lights?

💡 Click to View Step-by-Step Worked Solution

Step 1: Compute the total wire resistance (\(R_{\text{wire}}\)) Using the physical resistance equation \(R = \rho \frac{\ell}{A}\): \[R_{\text{wire}} = \left(1.72 \times 10^{-8}\ \Omega\cdot\text{m}\right) \frac{6.0\text{ m}}{2.08 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}^2} = 0.0496\ \Omega = \mathbf{49.6\text{ m}\Omega}\]

Step 2: Compute the line voltage drop (\(V_{\text{drop}}\)) By Ohm’s Law, the \(4.0\text{ A}\) current flowing through \(R_{\text{wire}}\) drops: \[V_{\text{drop}} = I \cdot R_{\text{wire}} = (4.0\text{ A})(0.0496\ \Omega) = \mathbf{0.198\text{ V}}\]

Step 3: Compute the voltage remaining at the spreader lights (\(V_{\text{load}}\)) \[V_{\text{load}} = V_{\text{battery}} - V_{\text{drop}} = 12.8\text{ V} - 0.198\text{ V} = \mathbf{12.60\text{ V}}\] (Note: Because \(0.198\text{ V} / 12.8\text{ V} = 1.55\%\), this is well below the ABYC 3% marine voltage drop limit for critical lighting!)

3 Ideal vs. Practical (Non-Ideal) Sources

In circuit schematics, we distinguish between ideal sources (theoretical constructs that maintain fixed voltage or current regardless of load) and practical (non-ideal) sources (real physical supplies like lab bench supplies, batteries, and solar arrays that exhibit internal losses).

3.1 Ideal vs. Practical Voltage Sources

  • Ideal Voltage Source (\(V_s\)): Maintains a fixed terminal voltage \(V_T = V_s\) regardless of the current \(I_L\) drawn from it. Its internal resistance is strictly zero (\(R_s = 0\ \Omega\)).
  • Practical Voltage Source: Modeled as an ideal voltage source \(V_s\) in series with an internal resistance \(R_s\). When a load resistor \(R_L\) is connected across its terminals, internal voltage drop occurs:

\[V_T = V_s - I_L R_s = V_s \left(\frac{R_L}{R_s + R_L}\right)\]

As the load demands more current (\(R_L\) decreases), the terminal voltage \(V_T\) droops.

3.2 Ideal vs. Practical Current Sources

  • Ideal Current Source (\(I_s\)): Supplies a fixed current \(I_L = I_s\) regardless of the voltage across its terminals. Its internal resistance is infinite (\(R_p \to \infty\)).
  • Practical Current Source: Modeled as an ideal current source \(I_s\) in parallel with an internal shunt resistance \(R_p\). Some current leaks internally through \(R_p\), reducing the current delivered to the load:

\[I_L = I_s - \frac{V_T}{R_p} = I_s \left(\frac{R_p}{R_p + R_L}\right)\]

4 Kirchhoff’s Laws & Series Circuits

To solve any linear circuit systematically, we rely on two foundational conservation laws published by Gustav Kirchhoff in 1845.

4.1 Definitions of Circuit Topology

  • Branch: A single two-terminal circuit element (such as a voltage source or a resistor).
  • Node: A point of connection between two or more branches. Every point along an ideal wire (with no components in between) belongs to the exact same node.
  • Loop: Any closed path in a circuit that starts at a node, passes through a set of nodes without visiting any intermediate node more than once, and returns to the starting node.

4.2 Kirchhoff’s Current Law (KCL)

Based on the Conservation of Electric Charge, KCL states that the algebraic sum of all currents entering and leaving any node (or closed boundary) at any instant is zero:

\[\sum_{n=1}^{N} i_n = 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \sum I_{\text{entering}} = \sum I_{\text{leaving}}\]

4.3 Kirchhoff’s Voltage Law (KVL)

Based on the Conservation of Energy, KVL states that the algebraic sum of all voltages around any closed loop in a circuit is zero:

\[\sum_{m=1}^{M} v_m = 0 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \sum V_{\text{rises}} = \sum V_{\text{drops}}\]

Tip🧭 Sign Convention for Walking a KVL Loop

Choose a starting corner (usually the bottom-left ground reference) and walk around the loop (typically clockwise). Each time you encounter a component, record the first polarity sign (\(+\) or \(-\)) you hit as you enter that component: * If you enter the \(-\) terminal and exit the \(+\) terminal, it is a voltage rise (\(-v\) if summing drops, or \(+v\) if summing rises). * If you enter the \(+\) terminal and exit the \(-\) terminal, it is a voltage drop. * Standard convention: Add (+) whenever you hit a \(+\) sign first; subtract (\(-\)) whenever you hit a \(-\) sign first, and set the sum equal to \(0\).

4.4 Series Resistors and Voltage Division

Two or more elements are in series if they share a single common node that has no other branching paths, forcing the exact same current \(i\) to flow through every element in sequence:

\[R_{\text{eq}} = R_1 + R_2 + \dots + R_N = \sum_{k=1}^{N} R_k\]

For \(N\) resistors in series connected across a net source voltage \(V_T\), the voltage across any individual resistor \(R_k\) is given directly by the Voltage Divider Rule (VDR):

\[V_k = V_T \left(\frac{R_k}{R_{\text{eq}}}\right)\]

5.1 Problem 1.1: Dual-Source Series Loop with Reference Polarity

Problem 1.1: Find \(i_x\) and \(v_x\) ELT 112 Exam 1 — Part I

Find the current \(i_x\) (in \(\text{mA}\)) and the voltage \(v_x\) (in \(\text{V}\)) in the circuit below. Pay careful attention to the polarity of the \(5\text{ V}\) and \(7\text{ V}\) sources!

💡 Click to View Step-by-Step Worked Solution & Circuitikz Code

Step 1: Apply KVL around the clockwise loop (\(i_x\)) Starting at the bottom-left corner and walking clockwise in the direction of \(i_x\): * Passing upward through the \(5\text{ V}\) source, we enter the \(+\) terminal and exit the \(-\) terminal: \(+5\text{ V}\). * Passing left-to-right through the \(7\text{ V}\) source, we enter the \(+\) terminal and exit the \(-\) terminal: \(+7\text{ V}\). * Passing downward through the \(100\ \Omega\) resistor, we enter the \(+\) terminal of \(v_x\): \(+v_x\).

\[\text{KVL: } +5\text{ V} + 7\text{ V} + v_x = 0 \implies v_x = \mathbf{-12\text{ V}}\]

Step 2: Apply Ohm’s Law to find \(i_x\) Since the reference arrow \(i_x\) enters the positive (\(+\)) terminal of \(v_x\), the Passive Sign Convention gives: \[i_x = \frac{v_x}{100\ \Omega} = \frac{-12\text{ V}}{100\ \Omega} = -0.12\text{ A} = \mathbf{-120\text{ mA}}\] (Physical interpretation: Because both sources have their positive terminals oriented to push current counter-clockwise, actual conventional current of \(120\text{ mA}\) flows counter-clockwise, opposite to \(i_x\).)

5.2 Problem 1.2: Multi-Source, Multi-Resistor Series Loop

Problem 1.2: Find the current \(i\) ELT 112 Exam 1 — Part I

Find the current \(i\) (in \(\text{mA}\)) in the following series circuit. Note the magenta reference arrow for \(i\) pointing to the left across the \(25\ \Omega\) resistor!

💡 Click to View Step-by-Step Worked Solution & Circuitikz Code

Step 1: Find Total Series Equivalent Resistance (\(R_T\)) Since this is a single-loop series circuit, all three resistors carry the exact same current: \[R_T = 15\ \Omega + 25\ \Omega + 5\ \Omega = 45\ \Omega\]

Step 2: Apply KVL in the direction of current \(i\) (Counter-Clockwise) Notice that the reference arrow \(i\) points to the left along the top branch (counter-clockwise). Let’s sum the voltage rises/drops walking counter-clockwise starting from the top-right corner: * Going right-to-left across \(25\ \Omega\) and \(15\ \Omega\): \(+i(25\ \Omega + 15\ \Omega)\) * Going right-to-left across the top \(5\text{ V}\) source (entering \(-\) and exiting \(+\)): \(-5\text{ V}\) * Going top-to-bottom across the left \(5\text{ V}\) source (entering \(-\) and exiting \(+\)): \(-5\text{ V}\) * Going left-to-right across the bottom \(5\ \Omega\) resistor: \(+i(5\ \Omega)\) * Going bottom-to-top across the right \(5\text{ V}\) source (entering \(-\) and exiting \(+\)): \(-5\text{ V}\)

\[i(15 + 25 + 5) - 5\text{ V} - 5\text{ V} - 5\text{ V} = 0\] \[45\,i = 15\text{ V} \implies i = \frac{15\text{ V}}{45\ \Omega} = \frac{1}{3}\text{ A} = \mathbf{333.33\text{ mA}}\]

5.3 Problem 1.3: Voltage Division in a Series Loop

Problem 1.3: Voltage Divider Rule ELT 112 Exam 1 — Part I

Calculate the magnitude of the voltage across the \(20\ \Omega\) resistor (\(V_{20}\)) using the Voltage Divider Rule.

💡 Click to View Step-by-Step Worked Solution & Circuitikz Code

Step 1: Compute the Total Series Resistance (\(R_{\text{eq}}\)) All three resistors (\(10\ \Omega\), \(20\ \Omega\), and \(30\ \Omega\)) are connected in a single series loop: \[R_{\text{eq}} = 10\ \Omega + 20\ \Omega + 30\ \Omega = 60\ \Omega\]

Step 2: Apply the Voltage Divider Rule \[V_{20} = V_s \left(\frac{R_{20}}{R_{\text{eq}}}\right) = 12\text{ V} \left(\frac{20\ \Omega}{60\ \Omega}\right) = 12\text{ V} \left(\frac{1}{3}\right) = \mathbf{4.0\text{ V}}\]

6 💻 Laboratory & Simulation Corner: Verifying Series Circuits in LTspice

Before moving to Chapter 2 (Series-Parallel Networks & Current Division), let’s connect the theory above to your virtual and hardware lab workflow:

  1. Ground Reference (\(0\text{ V}\)) Requirement: Every SPICE simulator (LTspice and TINA-TI) solves circuits using a Nodal Analysis matrix. If you draw Problem 1.3 in LTspice without placing a GND (G) symbol on the bottom wire, SPICE will throw a Singular Matrix: Check node... error because node voltages are undefined without a \(0\text{ V}\) reference!
  2. Measuring Differential Voltage (\(V_{20}\)): In both LTspice and on your bench Siglent SDS2204X Plus oscilloscope, nodal probes measure voltage with respect to ground. Notice that in Problem 1.3, neither terminal of the \(20\ \Omega\) resistor is connected directly to the bottom ground wire (the \(30\ \Omega\) resistor sits in between).
    • In LTspice: Click and hold on the node between the \(10\ \Omega\) and \(20\ \Omega\) resistors (red probe), drag to the node between the \(20\ \Omega\) and \(30\ \Omega\) resistors (black probe), and release.
    • On the Bench DMM: Connect the red V/Ω lead to the left lead of the \(20\ \Omega\) resistor and the black COM lead to the right lead of the \(20\ \Omega\) resistor (since a handheld or benchtop DMM has floating inputs, unlike a grounded-BNC oscilloscope).
