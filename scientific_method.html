The Scientific Method: Questions & Hypotheses

A Step-by-Step Guide to Student Research | K–12 STEM Outreach

Author

Wesley Francillon, PhD

← Back to K–12 Outreach

Part 1: The Big Picture (The Scientific Method)

Science isn’t just memorizing facts from a textbook—it is a structured way of testing ideas against reality. Whether you are building a science fair project, competing in a STEM challenge, or participating in an enrichment program such as STEP, every investigation follows the same six-step cycle.

Step 1 Observation & Background Notice a pattern, problem, or gap in existing knowledge and read what others have already discovered.

Step 2 • Focus Below Research Question Narrow a broad curiosity into one clear, focused, and measurable question.

Step 3 • Focus Below Testable Hypothesis Make an educated, falsifiable If–Then prediction before collecting any data.

Step 4 Controlled Experiment Change only one variable at a time while holding all other conditions constant.

Step 5 Data & Analysis Organize measurements into graphs and tables to see whether the evidence supports or rejects your prediction.

Step 6 Conclusion & Share Communicate your findings through a poster, report, or presentation—even if your hypothesis was disproven.

TipKey Takeaway for Student Researchers

Being “wrong” about your hypothesis does not mean your experiment failed. In science, disproving a hypothesis eliminates a dead end and moves knowledge forward—as long as your experiment was fair and controlled.

Part 2: Deep Dive — Questions & Hypotheses

The hardest part of any project is moving from a broad topic to a testable hypothesis. Great research follows a 4-stage narrowing pipeline:

1. Broad Topic vs. Focused Question

A common mistake in student projects is picking a question that is too huge to test in a single semester—or one that can be answered with a simple “yes” or “no.”

WarningCommon Pitfalls to Avoid
  • Too Broad: Covers an entire field without limits (e.g., “How does the environment affect people?”).
  • Wordy & Tangled: Mixes multiple problems into one messy question.
  • Binary (Yes/No): Stops deep thinking (e.g., “Is tool X good?”).
  • Leading / Biased: Assumes the answer before you even test it.
NoteCriteria for an Effective Question
  • Clear: Every term is defined—no vague words like “better” or “healthier.”
  • Concise: Focuses on one central issue.
  • Open-Ended: Starts with “How” or “Why” to explore relationships.
  • Addresses a Deficit: Targets something we don’t fully know yet.

Checking Your Question: The FINER Framework

Before spending time or money on equipment, test your question against these five criteria:

Letter Criterion What to Ask Yourself
F Feasible Do I have enough subjects, time, equipment, and budget to actually finish this?
I Interesting Will the answer genuinely intrigue you, your peers, and science fair judges?
N Novel Does this confirm, challenge, or extend what we already know?
E Ethical Is this completely safe and ethical for humans, animals, and the environment?
R Relevant Does the answer matter to real-world science, engineering, or health?
TipHigh School / Advanced Track: The PICOT Structure

If you are designing a health, sports science, or behavioral comparison study, researchers use PICOT to lock down every detail:

  • P – Population: Who is your exact test group? (e.g., high school varsity runners)
  • I – Intervention: What new treatment or factor are you testing? (e.g., 10 minutes of dynamic stretching)
  • C – Comparison: What are you comparing it against? (e.g., static stretching or no warm-up)
  • O – Outcome: What exact number will you measure? (e.g., 100-meter sprint time in seconds)
  • T – Time Frame: How long will the study run? (e.g., over 4 weeks)

Example Question: “How does daily dynamic stretching (I) compare with static stretching (C) in reducing 100m sprint times (O) among high school track athletes (P) over 4 weeks (T)?”

2. Isolating Variables & The Golden Rule

A strong experiment isolates the exact cause-and-effect relationship between two factors:

  • Independent Variable (X): The cause—the single condition you deliberately change or test.
  • Dependent Variable (Y): The effect—the outcome metric you measure during the experiment.
  • Controlled Variables: Everything else you keep 100% identical across all test groups so outside factors don’t ruin your results.
ImportantThe Golden Rule of Experimentation

Manipulate only ONE variable at a time.

If you change two things at once (for example, changing both fertilizer and sunlight on a plant), you will never know which one actually caused the plant to grow taller.

3. Formulating a Testable Hypothesis

A hypothesis is a formal, educated prediction written before you collect a single piece of data.

The Standard “If–Then” Format:

“If [Independent Variable X is changed in a specific way], then [Dependent Variable Y will respond in a predicted way].”

  • Directional: It states how you expect Y to change (increase, decrease, react faster).
  • Prospective: Writing it beforehand prevents “cherry-picking” patterns after looking at your data.
NoteHigh School / AP Statistics Track: Null (H_0) vs. Alternative (H_A) Hypotheses

When scientists run statistical tests on their data, they split their prediction into two competing statements:

  • Null Hypothesis (H_0): Assumes no real difference exists between your test groups (H_0: \mu_{\text{exp}} = \mu_{\text{ctrl}}). Any variation you see is just random luck.
  • Alternative Hypothesis (H_A): Assumes your change did make a real, systematic difference (H_A: \mu_{\text{exp}} \neq \mu_{\text{ctrl}}).

Your goal in an experiment is to gather enough evidence to see if you can reject H_0.

4. Real-World Case Studies

Explore how famous scientists and student researchers turned broad curiosities into testable hypotheses:

  • Video Games & Reflexes (Student Lab)
  • Genetics: Gregor Mendel (1865)
  • Astrochemistry: Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin (1925)
  • Broad Topic: Do video games improve reflexes?
  • Refined Question: How does routine weekly action video gameplay affect physical reaction latency during a standardized dropped-ruler catch task?
  • Variables:
    • Independent (X): Weekly action gameplay (\ge 5\text{ hrs/week} vs. 0\text{ hrs/week}).
    • Dependent (Y): Reaction latency measured in milliseconds (\text{ms}).
    • Controls: Identical drop height, ruler weight, starting hand position, and testing time of day.
  • If–Then Hypothesis: “If students play \ge 5 hours of action video games weekly, then they will catch a dropped ruler in fewer milliseconds than non-gamers.”
  • Statistical Framing:
    • H_0: \mu_{\text{gamer}} = \mu_{\text{non-gamer}} (no difference in reaction time)
    • H_A: \mu_{\text{gamer}} < \mu_{\text{non-gamer}} (gamers have shorter latency)
  • Broad Topic: How are physical traits passed from parents to offspring?
  • Refined Question: What is the exact mathematical ratio of discrete traits in the second-generation (F_2) offspring of cross-pollinated pea plants?
  • Variables:
    • Independent (X): Controlled parent cross-pollination pairs (purebred dominant vs. recessive lines).
    • Dependent (Y): Countable ratio of visible traits (e.g., tall vs. short stems) in F_2 plants.
    • Controls: Identical soil, water, sunlight, and species (Pisum sativum).
  • Hypothesis & Breakthrough: Mendel rejected the vague idea of “blended traits” and predicted that discrete factors segregate independently—yielding a predictable 3:1 trait ratio in the second generation.
  • Broad Topic: What are stars made of?
  • Refined Question: Does the relative abundance of chemical elements in stellar atmospheres—calculated from spectral lines—differ from Earth’s crust?
  • Variables:
    • Independent (X): Thermal ionization states modeled from starlight absorption spectra (Saha ionization equation).
    • Dependent (Y): Calculated percentage of each element in the star’s atmosphere.
  • Hypothesis & Breakthrough: While senior astronomers assumed stars had the same rock-forming elements as Earth, Payne-Gaposchkin’s calculations predicted and proved that stars are over 98\% hydrogen and helium.

5. Pre-Experiment Checklist

Before you start collecting data for your research project, verify all five checkpoints:

“A sensible strategy is to establish a single primary research question around which to focus the study plan.” — Brian Haynes / Farrugia et al. (2010)
